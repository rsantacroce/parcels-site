Getting started

This manual covers the single-player city builder coming in v0.3. Until it is released, build it from source; the v0.2 downloads are the earlier multiplayer edition.

Unpack the game and run parcels from inside its folder (it reads config/balance.ron from there and writes saves to saves/). On macOS, clear the download quarantine once with xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine parcels-*.

The title screen shows a city growing by itself in the background and offers:

  • Continue: the most recent save.
  • New city: the city's name, map size (64×48 to 256×256), rivers, lakes, woods, starting money, fires and seed, with a live preview of the map.
  • Load city: named saves with their date, population and treasury.
  • Settings: volume, mouse, shadows, day/night speed, cars and people, interface size, autosave.

The goal

There is no time limit and no score. Parcels is a sandbox: build the city you want. What keeps you honest is money (you can't build without cash in hand) and your residents, whose opinion of you shows up as the mayor's approval rating. The game cheers when the population passes 100, 500, 1,000, 2,500 and so on.

Regions

A city can stand alone, or be one of several in a region. Choose Regions on the title screen and create one: a grid of 2×2, 3×3 or 4×4 plots, each its own city map of the size you pick, with shared starting money, eras and fire settings. The region map shows every plot. Click open land to found a city there, or a city to play it. Back to the region in the Game menu saves the city and returns to the map; region cities also save themselves when you quit.

Neighbouring cities connect only where something crosses the map edge between them. Signs at the edges of the map name the city beyond, and the Region card shows what's linked:

  • A road lets residents commute to the neighbour's open jobs, and its job seekers drive in to fill yours. It also adds demand: for homes near their jobs, shops for their residents and factories for their job seekers.
  • A power line or water pipe buys the neighbour's spare power or water when your network falls short, and sells your spare to a neighbour that's short. Buying costs more than running your own plant; selling earns a little. The Budget card shows the trade.

Each city sees its neighbours as they were when you last played them, so play them in turn to keep the numbers fresh. Every plot has its own land: rivers don't continue across borders yet.

Eras

A new city starts as a Town: streets, small zones, coal and wind power, buses and the basic services. As it grows it moves into the City era (1,500 residents, or year 4 at the latest) with avenues, offices, gas and solar power, water treatment and civic landmarks; then Metropolis (6,000, or year 7) with dense zones, hospitals, universities and the stadium; then Modern (15,000, or year 11) with nuclear power and the airport. Locked items show in the build menus with a padlock and the era that brings them. The City card shows how close the next era is, and the game pauses to announce each new one. Untick Eras when founding a city to have everything from the start.

The screen

  • Left rail: Inspect, Zoning, Roads, Power, Water, Services, Parks, Landmarks, Demolish and Data views. Clicking a category opens a list of everything in it, with icons and prices.
  • Right rail: the Inbox (letters from residents, with a red count of open ones), the City card (approval and what residents think), the Budget card (money, tax rate, power and water), the Mayor card (your term, the next election, goodwill and past results), the isometric/3D camera switch, street view, the Region card (neighbouring cities and what crosses each border), help and the game menu (save, load, sound, quit).
  • Dashboard along the bottom: minimap (click or drag to move the camera), city name and date, speed buttons, the approval meter, treasury with net income per month, population, the next election (green if you'd win today, red if not), jobless rate, power and water status, the top issue and the RCIO demand chart.
  • Hover a building with Inspect to see what it is and how it's doing; click it to open the inspector.

Roads

Every building needs a road within 2 tiles. Roads can be laid across water as bridges, at four times the price.

ItemCost per tileNotes
Street$10Two lanes.
Avenue$25Planted median. Quicker, and carries more than twice the traffic of a street, so commuters seek it out.
Bus stop$150Beside a road. Covers 4 tiles around it once a depot runs its road network.
Bus depot$4,0002×2, beside a road, needs power. Runs buses on the road network it touches, up to 600 riders.

Traffic and commuting

Traffic isn't decoration: every few seconds the city works out how each resident gets to work. People take the quickest route along the roads to the nearest job that still has room, and a road's traffic is the number of trips crossing it. Jammed roads lower land value, add pollution and annoy residents. To fix a jam, upgrade the busiest streets to avenues, give people a second route, put jobs closer to homes, or run buses. Homes whose roads don't connect to any job count as out of work.

When a home and its workplace are both near a working bus stop, 40% of those commuters take the bus instead of driving. The City card's Getting around section shows the average commute, how many ride the bus and anyone with no road to work. Bus fares show in the Budget card.

Zones and growth

You don't build houses, shops or factories yourself. You zone land, and buildings move in when there is demand. The RCIO chart in the dashboard shows demand for residential, commercial, industrial and office.

A lot grows one level at a time. Low-density zones stop at level 3, dense zones go up to level 6. Every eligible lot competes for the same pool of newcomers, and the lots with the highest land value win.

Power and water

Power lines and water pipes cost $5 per tile. Zones and most buildings pass power and water on to their neighbours, so a block of zoned tiles only needs one connection. Parks, plazas, playgrounds and monuments don't conduct.

Every connected network shares its supply. If demand outstrips it, the tiles at the far end go without, and the dashboard's Power & water row turns red. Pumps need power to pump, so they are served first. Pipes can follow a bridge across the river.

Money

The exact formulas and a revenue calculator are in the Parcelspedia. Each month you collect taxes from your buildings and pay upkeep for everything you built. The Budget card shows both, and the dashboard shows the net per month next to the treasury.

  • Tax rate (0–20%, default 7%, set in the Budget card). Above 7% demand falls and residents grumble; below it demand rises and they're pleased.
  • Debt. Building needs cash in hand. Upkeep can push your treasury below zero, but never below −$5,000.
  • Land value (0–1000) comes from parks, the river, shops, services and utilities, minus pollution and traffic, spread by distance.

Your residents

Residents rate nine parts of city life, judged from what the homes around the city actually experience, weighted by how many people live there:

FactorBetter when
JobsThere are enough jobs for everyone (the dashboard shows the jobless rate)
Power & waterNo home goes without
SafetyCrime is low; police stations cut it
Clean airIndustry and power plants are kept away from homes
Schools & healthHomes are covered by a school and a clinic or hospital
TaxesThe rate is at or below 7%
TrafficStreets near homes aren't jammed; avenues help
NeighbourhoodsLand value is high: parks, the river, shops, services
City financesThe treasury isn't in debt

Their sum sets the mayor's approval (0–100%), which moves gradually so one bad month doesn't swing it. The City card shows each factor as a bar, a mood word (Thriving, Happy, Content, Uneasy, Unhappy, Angry) and the top issue: the factor dragging approval down most, in your residents' words.

Being mayor

Letters. Now and then a resident writes in, usually about the city's top issue. Each letter asks for one concrete thing and gives you twelve months:

  • a school, clinic, police station or fire station covering their home;
  • a park, plaza, playground or sports field within 4 tiles of their home;
  • taxes back down to 7%, no homes without power or water, fewer people out of work, or the city out of debt;
  • cleaner air or quieter streets at their home.

The Inbox lists open letters with how long is left; Show moves the camera to the writer's home. A letter is granted the moment its goal is met, which earns goodwill worth about 6% approval. One that runs out, or whose writer's home you bulldoze, costs about 4%. Goodwill fades over the following months. At most three letters are open at once.

Elections come every four years. You need 50% approval on polling day to win. If you lose, you can stay on as caretaker and keep building, or go back to the title screen. The Mayor card marks the 50% line on your approval meter and lists past results.

Approval matters for growth. Above 50% it adds to residential demand and below 50% it takes away, so an unpopular mayor sees homes empty out.

Data views

Number keys 1–9 and 0 (or Data views on the left rail) tint the map: city, land value, power, water, density, pollution, traffic, crime, services and buses. The traffic view colours each road from blue (quiet) through orange (at capacity) to red (jammed); the buses view shows stops and the area they reach. Buildings fade to grey so the ground colours read clearly. The water view also shows pipes.

Saves and settings

Saves live in saves/ next to the game: named saves from the menu, quicksave.ron (F5/F9) and an autosave whose interval you set in Settings. A save keeps the balance rules it was started with, so tweaking config/balance.ron only affects new games. Saves from v0.2 (the multiplayer edition) can't be loaded in v0.3.

Command line

parcels                                      # title screen
parcels --new --map 128x96 --city "New Harbor"  # straight into a new city
parcels --load quicksave                     # load a save by name or path
parcels --seed 42 --name Rob                 # fixed map seed, mayor's name
parcels --verify-replay saves/replay.ron     # check a replay reproduces exactly

Tips

  • Start small: a short street, a block of residential and industrial, a wind turbine or coal plant, a water tower. Add commercial once you have residents.
  • Lots by the river get a land value bonus, and pumps there make far more water.
  • Keep heavy industry and coal plants well away from your best housing: pollution costs land value and approval.
  • Watch the top issue in the dashboard. It tells you, in plain words, what to build next.
  • Parks are the cheapest land value there is. Scatter them through your residential blocks.
  • Dense zones need land value 380 or more before they build towers. Prepare the land with parks and services first.
